# Pitch range before depth

**Use when:** the request names a subject but not an idea ("a launch video for the new release", "something for the reel"), whether a human is in the loop or the agent is running on its own.

## The problem this solves

Ask a model for a concept and the first answer is the most probable answer, which is the one every other team will also get. Every later step (variant rounds, sliders, polish) only improves the concept it starts from; none of them can swap it for a better one. So the cheapest moment to be original is before anything is animated. Spend it on breadth: several concepts that disagree about what the piece is, then commit to one.

## Step 1: spread across levers

Write five one-line concepts. Each one should pull a different lever, so they disagree in kind rather than in polish:

| lever | the concept asks |
|---|---|
| literal | What if the viewer simply watches the thing do its job, beautifully staged? |
| stand-in object | Which single physical object could play the product's role (a key, a lens, a thread)? |
| borrowed container | Which familiar form could hold the story: a receipt, a flight board, a recipe card, a subway map, a ticker? |
| point of view | Whose eyes are these: the user's, the data's, a bystander's, the machine's? |
| genre reversal | List what videos on this topic always do (dark gradients, glowing particles, a big logo slam), then build the piece that does the opposite. |

Each concept then has to pass three reality checks against the brief. Does it use something only this subject has: its tools, its materials, its jargon made visible? Does it suit how it will be watched: scrolled past, sat through, or looping in the background? And can you name the one frame a viewer would remember, the frame that carries the intended mood?

## Step 2: three filters, all private

**Shape filter.** Rough out each concept as a thumbnail: four or five grey blocks showing where the big masses sit in its key frame. When two thumbnails match, the concepts are variants of one idea. Keep the stronger one and write a new concept for the freed slot.

**Structure filter.** Write each concept as a beat list: the five or six things that happen, in order. Concepts must differ in structure, not just in skin. Two concepts with the same beat list in different costumes (open on the problem, three feature beats, logo) are one concept reskinned: keep the stronger and write a new one. Then run the **swap test** on every survivor: replace the subject's name and words with another brand's. If the beat list still works, it is a template, not a concept for this subject. Reject it and rebuild it around something only this subject has.

**Likelihood filter.** Put a rough number on each surviving concept: out of a hundred agents given this same request, how many would land on it? A set passes when at least two of its concepts would come from fewer than ten of those hundred. Otherwise it is too safe: drop the most predictable ideas, write fresh ones and score again.

These guesses steer the generation and stay internal. The human never sees the numbers.

## Step 3: offer the set

Present all five together, as a table, before saying which one you favour; an early favourite colours how the rest are read.

| concept | what you would see | the first two seconds |
|---|---|---|
| one sentence | its visual world, plus the one or two techniques it depends on, in plain words ("the camera flies through each headline") | how it opens |

After the table, say which concept you would make and why. The human may splice two together (the container of one, the opening of another); treat that as a good outcome, not a compromise. This is a one-shot offer: generate more only on request.

**If the human already has an idea,** it joins the set as a concept in its own right and stays in until they drop it. If what they describe is already complete, skip the round: their idea is the concept.

## Running without a human

Skip nothing. Without a reviewer, nothing else stops the predictable idea from shipping, so the filters matter more here, not less. Choose the concept yourself and leave a short note with the result: what you built, why, and the safe idea you considered and rejected, so the reader can still ask for it.
